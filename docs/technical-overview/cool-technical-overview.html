1. Executive summary

Software, and increasingly AI agents, now take actions that matter: they change code and configuration, deploy systems, move money, and edit the instructions that steer other AI systems. When something goes wrong, or when a regulator, customer or court asks what happened, the usual answer is a log file. A log is written and stored by the same organisation whose conduct is in question. It can be edited, and nobody outside can tell.

CooL makes such records checkable by anyone. Each action becomes a receipt: a small record of what was done, to what, by whom, on whose request and with what result. The receipt is digitally signed and given a sequence number. Receipts are then sealed into a bundle that another party can verify on their own computer, without a network connection and without trusting the producer. The verifier gives one of three answers:

AnswerMeaning
VALIDThe record has not been altered, nothing was removed from the middle, nothing was reordered or duplicated, and it was signed by the key the verifier was told to expect.
INVALIDSomething was checked and found wrong, and the verifier says what.
INDETERMINATENothing wrong was found, but something could not be decided. Usually the verifier was not told independently which key to trust.

What CooL proves

  • The recorded history was not changed after the fact.
  • Records were not silently removed from the middle, reordered or replayed.
  • Who signed them, using post-quantum cryptography.

What it does not prove

  • That every action was recorded: only those that passed through CooL.
  • That declared identities (which agent, which person) are true.
  • That a policy was followed. CooL records it; it does not enforce it, unless its enforcement component is used.

Where it stands. The open core library is published on npm under Apache-2.0, and its hardware attestation verifiers are validated against genuine evidence from AWS Nitro, Intel TDX and AMD SEV-SNP. The integration for AI tools, released in November 2026, works with any client that supports the Model Context Protocol, including Claude Code, Claude Desktop, Cursor, Windsurf, VS Code, Codex CLI and Gemini CLI. It is labelled experimental until it has been run in more clients. No independent security audit has been carried out yet; the review package is ready.

2. The problem

Three things have changed at the same time.

  1. Software acts on its own. AI agents write and deploy code, answer customers, approve refunds and call other systems. They act quickly, often, and without a person checking each step.
  2. Records are asked for more often. Regulators in many countries now expect organisations to keep logs of how automated and AI systems behave (§12). Customers and auditors ask for evidence, not assurances.
  3. Ordinary logs cannot settle a dispute. A log proves only what its keeper says it proves. An administrator, an attacker, or a well-meaning cleanup script can change or delete entries. Nobody outside the organisation can tell.
In plain words. Today, "our logs show the agent did nothing wrong" is a claim you have to take on trust. CooL turns it into something the other side can check for themselves.

Existing tools address parts of this. Write-once storage stops casual deletion, but the storage owner can still rewrite it, and nobody outside can check. Monitoring tools show behaviour but sign nothing. Blockchains are public, slow and expensive, and they disclose more than most organisations can share. §14 compares the approaches.

4. How it works, in five steps

  1. Record. When an agent acts through CooL, CooL writes down what was done (for example "edited CLAUDE.md" or "deployed payments-api"), to what, by which agent, on whose request, under which approval, and the outcome. The actual content (the prompt, the file, the customer data) is not stored. Only a keyed fingerprint of it is.
  2. Sign. The receipt is signed twice: once with ML-DSA-65, the post-quantum standard published by NIST in 2024, and once with Ed25519, the widely deployed classic algorithm. Both must check out, so the receipt stays secure even if one algorithm is broken in future.
  3. Number. Receipts are numbered without gaps. If one is later removed from the middle, the gap is visible.
  4. Seal. Receipts are placed in a Merkle tree, the structure used by Certificate Transparency, which protects the web's security certificates. The tree's summary (its "root") is signed. Each receipt gets a short proof that it is in that tree. Everything is written to a bundle: a folder of plain files.
  5. Verify. Anyone with the bundle and the producer's fingerprint (a short code obtained separately, for example on a call) runs one command. The answer is VALID, INVALID or INDETERMINATE, with a reason for every check.
In plain words. It works like a numbered, signed receipt book whose pages are bound together. You can still tear out a page or write over one, but the reader will see it.

5. A worked example

A developer uses an AI coding assistant on a payments service. Over an afternoon, the assistant:

  • edits CLAUDE.md, the file of standing instructions for the assistant;
  • opens a pull request through a GitHub tool, which is routed through the CooL proxy;
  • deploys the service under change ticket CHG-2026-0142.

CooL writes three receipts, numbered 0, 1 and 2. At the end of the day the team lead runs seal. Weeks later an incident review asks: "Did the assistant change its own instructions before the deployment, and was the deployment approved?" The reviewer receives the bundle by email and the fingerprint on a call. One command answers VALID, with the three receipts in order, the instruction change before the deployment, and the ticket reference attached.

Now suppose someone had quietly changed the deployment's outcome from "failure" to "success" in the bundle, and even updated the file checksum to hide it. The same command answers INVALID: the receipt's signature no longer matches its content. The project's demo does exactly this, live, in under three minutes.

What the reviewer still cannot know from CooL alone: whether the assistant also did something that did not pass through CooL, such as a shell command; whether "user:alice" really was Alice; and whether the change ticket was genuinely approved. Each verification result states these limits in writing (§9).

6. Architecture

ComponentRoleLicence
Open core (cool-nwc)Evidence envelope, hybrid signatures, transparency log, bundles, verifiers for hardware attestation (Nitro, TDX, SEV-SNP), trust snapshots, the authorisation protocol and ToolGateway enforcementApache-2.0
MCP integration (mcp-server-cool)MCP server, proxy, Claude Code hook and plugin, Claude Desktop extension, offline verifier CLI, SIEM exportApache-2.0
Commercial platformOperating CooL across an organisation: control plane, policy lifecycle, cross-system correlation, retention, and a planned independent witness serviceCommercial

The open-core boundary. Everything needed to produce and to independently verify evidence is open source. A verifier must never depend on a paid product, or the evidence would not be independent. The commercial layer adds operation at scale, not trust.

For engineers. Capture writes one signed cool.envelope.v1 per line to .cool/mcp/receipts.jsonl, using O_APPEND plus fsync under a cross-process lock file. The lock is PID-aware, so a lock left by a crashed writer is broken. Sealing builds an RFC 6962 tree over envelope leaves, signs one tree head, and attaches an inclusion proof to every envelope. Then exportBundle signs a manifest over the sorted envelope digests. The MCP server uses the official TypeScript MCP SDK over stdio. The proxy is a low-level MCP Server that forwards tools/list and tools/call to a downstream Client.

7. The Agent Execution Receipt

The receipt format, cool.agent-receipt.v1, is published as a JSON Schema with an example and a sealed test bundle. Its fields:

FieldWhat it saysTrust level
event_id, timestampA unique identifier, and when the action was recordedclock of the recording machine
agent_idWhich agent acted, e.g. agent:claude-codedeclared
initiator_idWho asked for it, or the literal unknowndeclared
authority_refThe approval or ticket relied ondeclared
policy_versionThe policy said to be in forcedeclared; not enforced
tool_name, target_resourceWhat was done, and to whatrecorded
outcomesuccess, failure, denied, error or unknownrecorded
sequence_numberIts position: epoch.number, without gapssigned
captureHow it was captured: tool, proxy, hook, SDK, command linerecorded
payloadKeyed digests of input and output; raw_payload_stored: falsecomputed
signerKey, algorithm (ML-DSA-65 + Ed25519), fingerprintcryptographic
evidenceDigest of the signed envelope behind this viewcryptographic
"Declared" matters. If the agent says it acted for Alice, the receipt proves the agent said so at the time, and that nobody changed it since. It does not prove Alice asked. To bind identity strongly, use CooL's authorisation protocol, which has issuer-signed principals and delegation, or connect your identity provider.

Privacy by design

Prompts, inputs, outputs and file contents never enter a receipt. Each is reduced to an HMAC-SHA-256 digest under a random key that never leaves the workspace. Someone holding a bundle cannot test a guess ("was the input X?") without that key. The workspace owner can later prove that a particular input matched by disclosing it, and the key, to the person who needs to see it. This supports data-minimisation duties under data protection law (§12), but it is not a complete privacy assessment.

For engineers. digest = HMAC-SHA-256(commit_key, "cool.mcp.input.v1\0" ‖ canonical_json(value)). Canonical JSON sorts keys and drops whitespace. The envelope is canonical CBOR and is the signed object; the receipt is a derived view carrying evidence.envelope_digest.
Versioning rule: v1 is frozen. Adding a field creates v2, which v1 verifiers report as unsupported, never as verified. Verifiers keep accepting every older version. Every change ships with a schema, an example and a golden test vector.

8. Verification

Verification recomputes everything from the bytes. It needs no network and trusts nothing in the bundle that the bundle cannot prove about itself.

CheckQuestion it answersIf it fails
Components presentAre all files there and readable?missing / corrupt component
File hashesHas any file changed since sealing?file hash mismatch
Trust sourceWhich keys should be trusted, according to someone other than the bundle?fingerprint mismatch, or INDETERMINATE
Bundle manifest and exporterDo the contents match the signed list, and was it exported by the pinned key?manifest mismatch; exporter untrusted
Receipt signatures and proofsDoes every receipt's signature verify, and is every receipt in the signed tree?envelope failed
SequenceAre the numbers contiguous?sequence gap
DuplicatesIs anything included twice (a replay)?duplicate evidence
OrderDoes the numbering agree with the order in the tree?order mismatch
Tree headDo all receipts prove membership of the same tree the manifest names?tree head mismatch

Why the fingerprint is sent separately

A forger can create a perfectly consistent bundle signed with their own key. What they cannot do is make it match the fingerprint the genuine producer gave you. That is why the fingerprint travels by a different channel (a call, a signed letter, a published web page), and why, without it, the honest answer is INDETERMINATE, not VALID. Organisations that distribute key lists can supply a trust file instead.

Every result also states, in words, what the receipts cover and what they cannot show (§9). That text is part of the output, so it travels with the verdict.

Not detected: the capture boundary

These are not defects. They are the limits of any recorder that runs beside the system it records, and CooL states them rather than hiding them.

  1. Actions that bypass CooL. If an agent runs a shell command or calls a service that is not routed through the CooL proxy, there is no receipt. The proxy is the strongest capture mode because the agent does not have to choose to record.
  2. The hook sees selected files and tools. By default it records edits to prompt, agent, policy and model-configuration files made through the editor's file tools. A file changed by a shell command is not seen.
  3. CooL not running. Nothing is recorded while CooL is stopped.
  4. The last receipts removed before sealing. Removing receipts from the end leaves no gap. Detecting it needs a tree size held by someone else, such as an earlier bundle or a witness.
  5. A producer that rewrites its whole history. Keys live on the recording machine. Someone with full control of it can create a new, consistent history. An independent witness that keeps earlier tree heads closes this gap; it is planned. The core library already verifies witness co-signatures and RFC 3161 timestamps, which give independent proof of time.
  6. Declared facts may be untrue (§7).
  7. Clock. Times come from the recording machine unless a trusted timestamp is added.
Words to avoid. CooL is not "tamper-proof", does not produce a "complete audit trail", and is not "compliance certified". The accurate words are tamper-evident, covering actions recorded through CooL, and independently verifiable.

Raising assurance

Each step below closes one of the gaps listed above:

  • Route tools through the proxy rather than relying on the agent to record.
  • Keep or publish fingerprints and earlier tree sizes outside the producing organisation.
  • Add RFC 3161 timestamps from a trusted authority.
  • Hold keys in a cloud KMS or HSM (signers are available; their status is in §13).
  • Run the recorder in a hardware-isolated enclave whose attestation CooL verifies.
  • Use ToolGateway so that actions which bypass approval cannot execute at all.

12. Regulatory context

Not legal advice. This section summarises, at a high level, public rules that ask for records of automated or AI activity, as understood by Northwind Cipher on 18 November 2026. Laws and timetables change, and several items below were changing during 2025–2026. Confirm the current text with qualified counsel in each jurisdiction. Using CooL does not by itself make any organisation compliant with any law or standard.

Across jurisdictions, the same three needs recur. CooL addresses the first and the third directly, and the second partly:

  1. Keep records of how automated systems behaved.
  2. Protect personal data while doing so.
  3. Be able to show those records are reliable when asked.

United Kingdom

  • UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018 require accountability: being able to demonstrate compliance (Article 5(2)), data minimisation, and appropriate security. The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 amended rules on automated decision-making.
  • The UK regulates AI largely through existing sector regulators (the ICO, the FCA, the CMA, Ofcom) applying cross-sector principles, rather than through a single AI statute. Financial firms are also subject to FCA record-keeping and operational-resilience requirements.

Where CooL helps: evidence that records of automated actions were not altered, and receipts that hold digests rather than personal data. What remains yours: lawful basis, data protection impact assessments, retention schedules, human review.

Switzerland

  • The revised Federal Act on Data Protection (revFADP) has applied since 1 September 2023. It covers privacy by design and default, and information about automated individual decisions.
  • In February 2025 the Federal Council chose sector-specific adjustments over a horizontal "AI Act", and on 27 March 2025 it signed the Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI. A consultation draft of the implementing legislation was expected around the end of 2026; a more recent official page indicates spring 2027.
  • FINMA Guidance 08/2024 sets out expectations for governance and risk management when supervised institutions use AI, including documentation and traceability.

Where CooL helps: traceability of AI-driven actions that a supervisor or auditor can check independently. Hosting is on your own infrastructure; nothing is sent to Northwind Cipher.

Denmark and the European Union

  • The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) requires high-risk AI systems to allow automatic recording of events (Article 12), and requires providers and deployers to keep those logs, generally for at least six months (Articles 19 and 26). Following the Digital Omnibus on AI, which entered into force in July 2026, the high-risk obligations for stand-alone (Annex III) systems are reported to apply from 2 December 2027, and those for AI in regulated products (Annex I) from 2 August 2028. Most transparency duties under Article 50 applied from 2 August 2026. Check the Official Journal text for exact dates.
  • The GDPR applies throughout. NIS2 (Directive (EU) 2022/2555, implemented in Danish law) covers security and incident handling for essential and important entities. DORA (Regulation (EU) 2022/2554) has applied to financial entities since 17 January 2025 and covers ICT risk and incident records.
  • Denmark adopted national rules in 2025 designating its AI Act authorities. The Danish Data Protection Agency (Datatilsynet) supervises the GDPR.

Where CooL helps: Article 12-style event records that are tamper-evident and verifiable by a notified body, a market surveillance authority or a customer. What remains yours: risk management, conformity assessment, retention for the required period, and human oversight. CooL records; it does not classify your system's risk.

India

  • The Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 is being brought into force in phases by the DPDP Rules 2025, notified in November 2025. Most obligations on data fiduciaries apply 18 months after notification, in around May 2027.
  • CERT-In Directions of 28 April 2022 require covered entities to keep ICT system logs securely for 180 days within India, and to report specified cyber incidents within six hours.
  • The Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology published India AI Governance Guidelines in November 2025. Sector regulators, including the Reserve Bank of India, have issued their own expectations on responsible AI.

Where CooL helps: logs whose integrity can be shown during an incident inquiry, stored wherever you run CooL, including within India, and holding digests rather than personal data.

United States

  • There is no single federal AI law. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, 2023) and its Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1, 2024) are widely used voluntary frameworks that emphasise documentation and traceability.
  • Sector rules require reliable records: SEC Rule 17a-4 for broker-dealer records, including an audit-trail alternative to write-once storage since the 2022 amendments; HIPAA audit controls (45 CFR 164.312(b)); and Sarbanes-Oxley internal controls.
  • States are active. Colorado's AI Act (SB 24-205) was postponed in 2025 to 30 June 2026, with further changes debated; check its current status. Federal policy on state AI laws was also changing in 2025–2026.
  • NIST's post-quantum standards (FIPS 204, ML-DSA) and the federal migration timelines are a reason to sign long-lived records with post-quantum algorithms now.

International standards

ISO/IEC 42001:2023 (AI management systems) and ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (control 8.15, Logging) ask for logs to be produced, protected and reviewed. SOC 2 asks for evidence that controls operated. CooL supplies evidence for such controls; certification remains a matter between you and your auditor.

Write-once (WORM) storageStops ordinary deletionThe storage owner can still replace data; outsiders cannot check; no signer identity

CooL is designed to sit alongside these tools: it exports to your SIEM, verifies hardware attestation, and anchors in transparency logs. What it adds is a record of consequential actions that a third party can check.

15. Getting started and running a pilot

In an AI tool, in one line

claude mcp add cool -- npx -y @northwindcipher/mcp-server-cool --workspace "$PWD"

Equivalent configurations for Claude Desktop, Cursor, Windsurf, VS Code, Codex CLI and Gemini CLI are in docs/mcp.md. Until the npm package is published, the same commands work from a source checkout.

Verify a bundle someone sent you

npx -y @northwindcipher/mcp-server-cool verify ./bundle --fingerprint sha256:…
