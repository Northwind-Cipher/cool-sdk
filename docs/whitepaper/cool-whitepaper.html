CooL: A Verifiable Evidence System for Software and AI Agents

Northwind Cipher

github.com/Northwind-Cipher/cool-sdk

November 2026 · describes cool-nwc 4.1.0 and @northwindcipher/mcp-server-cool 0.1.0

Abstract. When software acts on someone's behalf, the record of what happened is usually kept by the same party whose conduct is in question. Examples include an AI agent editing code, a pipeline deploying a release, and a service issuing a refund. Logs can be edited, truncated or rewritten, and anyone who needs certainty must trust the operator. We propose a system in which each consequential action leaves a receipt that anyone can verify offline, without an account and without trusting the operator. Receipts are encoded canonically, signed with a hybrid post-quantum signature and numbered without gaps. They are then sealed into a Merkle transparency log whose signed tree heads can be co-signed by independent witnesses and anchored to external time. Inputs and outputs are never stored, only keyed digests of them. An authorization protocol can bind each action to the authority under which it was requested, and an enforcement gateway that owns the tool refuses what it cannot verify. Agents reach the system through the Model Context Protocol, so any MCP-capable AI tool can produce receipts. Verification never reports a gap as success. It answers valid, invalid or indeterminate, gives a reason for every check, and states in every result what the evidence does not cover.

1. Introduction

Organisations increasingly let software take consequential actions. An agent rewrites the instructions that steer another agent, opens a pull request, deploys a service or refunds a customer. When something goes wrong, the questions are always the same. Who did this? Were they allowed to? Under which rules? What exactly happened? Is the record complete?

Today those questions are answered from logs, and logs have a structural weakness: they are written, stored and served by the operator. A log can show an auditor one history and an investigator another. It can lose the one entry that matters, or be edited after the fact. Even an honest operator cannot prove that nothing was removed. Signing log lines helps only a little, because the operator holds the keys. Timestamps help only a little, because the operator sets the clock. Every assurance about software behaviour therefore reduces to trust in an institution. That trust is hardest to extend exactly when it is most needed: after an incident, in a dispute, or towards a regulator.

What is needed is an evidence system based on verification rather than trust. It must let any party check a record independently and offline, against keys they choose to trust. It must make alteration, omission and reordering detectable, bind an action to the authority under which it was taken, and report uncertainty honestly instead of collapsing it into success. This paper describes such a system. It does not prove that software made a good decision; nothing can. It proves what was recorded, by whom, under what declared authority, and that the record has not been changed, reordered, replayed or pruned from the middle since.

2. Overview

The life of a receipt has five steps (Figure 1). The first four happen where the work happens. The fifth happens wherever the reader is: an auditor, a customer, a regulator or a court.

A useful picture is a numbered receipt book whose pages are bound together and signed. Nothing prevents someone from tearing out a page or writing over one. What the system guarantees is that a reader will see that it happened. The rest of this paper explains how each step achieves that, and where its guarantee ends.

3. Evidence Envelopes

We define a unit of evidence as an envelope. Its core is a typed record containing:

  • an identifier, an event type and a payload schema;
  • the time the event occurred and the time it was observed;
  • the source system, the actor, the action and the resource;
  • references to earlier evidence;
  • an optional sequence number and attestation reference.

Payloads are typed and strictly validated. Fifty-one event families are defined, among them authorization requests and decisions, tool invocations, deployments, model inference and, new in this version, the agent execution receipt (Section 9).

The core is serialised with the deterministic encoding of CBOR [3], so a record has exactly one byte representation. The envelope digest is a SHA-256 hash over a domain label and those bytes. The signature covers a second, distinct label and the digest:

d = SHA-256("cool.envelope.v1\0" ‖ CBORCDE(core))     m = "cool.envelope.sig.v1\0" ‖ d

Envelopes refer to each other by digest. A decision references the request it answers, and a confirmation references the decision it executed (Figure 2). Order between them is therefore established by hashes, which nobody can backdate, rather than by timestamps, which anyone can set.

Figure 3 shows how one action becomes one envelope. Descriptive fields are carried as they are. Inputs and outputs pass through a keyed hash first and are never carried themselves (Section 15). The result is encoded, hashed and signed.

4. Hybrid Signatures

The signature is a pair. It holds an ML-DSA-65 signature [4] and an Ed25519 signature [5] over the same message, and a verifier accepts it only if both verify (Figure 4):

σ = ( ML-DSA-65.Sign(sk1, m), Ed25519.Sign(sk2, m) )     accept ⇔ Verify1 ∧ Verify2

Forging an envelope therefore requires breaking both schemes. The classical scheme protects against an undiscovered flaw in the newer lattice scheme. The lattice scheme, standardised by NIST in 2024, protects against a future quantum computer, which would break Ed25519. Records that may be needed in evidence a decade from now should be signed against both threats today.

Every signed object in the system carries its own domain label: envelopes, decisions, delegations, confirmations, principal records and tree heads. A signature made for one kind of object can therefore never be replayed as a signature on another.

5. Sequence Numbers

A signature shows that a record was not altered. It cannot show that a record is missing. For that, each producer numbers its receipts within an epoch: e.0, e.1, e.2, …, with no gaps. A verifier that sees e.0, e.1 and e.3 reports that e.2 is missing, rather than presenting the history as clean (Figure 5). The numbering is inside the signed core, so it cannot be renumbered to close the gap without breaking every later signature.

One case remains: removing the last receipts leaves no gap. Section 7 and Section 17 explain how an outside copy of the tree size closes that window.

6. Transparency Log

Signatures prevent alteration and numbers reveal gaps, but neither stops a producer from keeping two consistent histories and showing each to a different party. To make history append-only and globally consistent, every envelope digest becomes a leaf of a Merkle tree, following Certificate Transparency [6]:

leaf = SHA-256(0x00 ‖ d)     node = SHA-256(0x01 ‖ left ‖ right)

The log signs a tree head: its identifier, the tree size, the root hash and a timestamp (Figure 6). Sealing a batch requires one tree-head signature, not one per receipt.

With the tree head comes an inclusion proof for each envelope. This is the short path of sibling hashes from its leaf to the root (Figure 7). For a tree of n receipts the path has ⌈log2 n⌉ hashes: ten for a thousand receipts, twenty for a million. Changing any receipt changes its leaf, every hash above it and the root, and the signed tree head no longer matches. Given two tree heads, a consistency proof shows that the larger tree extends the smaller without changing any earlier leaf. A log that rewrites or forks its history therefore produces two heads that cannot be proven consistent, and anyone holding both has proof of misbehaviour.

7. Witnesses and Time

A consistency proof convicts a forking log only if someone compares the tree heads. Witnesses do that comparing. A witness is an independent party that receives each new tree head, checks that it extends the last head it accepted, and only then adds its own co-signature. A verifier can demand co-signatures from a threshold of witnesses it trusts. To show different histories to different parties, a log must then corrupt that many witnesses or partition them permanently. The guarantee depends on the witnesses being operated by different organisations. That is an operational property that cryptography cannot supply, and we state it as such.

Timestamps inside an envelope are assertions by its source. Where trusted time matters, a tree head or digest is anchored externally. An RFC 3161 time-stamp token [7] is verified offline against a pinned authority. Alternatively, an OpenTimestamps proof [8] commits the digest into a Bitcoin block [1]. Either turns "the operator says this existed then" into "a third party proves this existed by then". The same mechanism closes the window on removed final receipts. Once a tree size has been witnessed or anchored, a later bundle that is smaller, or inconsistent with it, is caught.

8. Trust and the Fingerprint

A valid signature says only that some key signed some bytes. Whether that key should be believed is a separate question, and we keep the two apart. A verifier supplies a trust snapshot: a dated list of keys, each with an owner, the purposes it is accepted for, a validity window and a status. A key trusted only to sign tree heads cannot sign a decision. Nothing is fetched at verification time. Two verifiers with the same evidence and the same snapshot always reach the same result.

A bundle cannot vouch for its own keys. A forger can produce a perfectly consistent bundle signed with keys of their own. The producer therefore publishes a short fingerprint, a SHA-256 hash over its public receipt and log keys, through a channel separate from the bundle (Figure 8). The verifier recomputes the fingerprint from the keys in the bundle and compares. Without a fingerprint or a trust snapshot from an independent source, the honest result is indeterminate, never valid.

fp = SHA-256("cool.mcp.fingerprint.v1" ‖ pkML-DSA ‖ pkEd25519 ‖ pklogML-DSA ‖ pklogEd25519)

Keys change over time. A rotated key is refused for signatures dated after its successor took over. A revoked or compromised key raises a subtler problem: its holder can claim any date, so a claimed date proves nothing. Such a signature is accepted only with independent proof that it existed before the revocation, such as inclusion in a checkpoint or an external time anchor. A snapshot may declare a maximum age. When it is older than that, the result is stale rather than verified.

9. Agent Execution Receipts

AI agents need one receipt with a stable meaning, whichever tool they use. The Agent Execution Receipt, cool.agent-receipt.v1, is the readable view of one signed agent.execution envelope. It is published as a JSON Schema with an example and a sealed test bundle that every future version must keep verifying.

Declared has a precise meaning. If the agent says it acted for Alice, the receipt proves the agent said so at the time, and that nobody has changed it since. It does not prove that Alice asked. Stronger identity comes from the authorization protocol (Section 11), in which an identity issuer signs who a principal is and every delegation is signed by the delegator.

The versioning rule is strict. Version 1 is frozen. Adding a field creates version 2, which a version-1 verifier reports as unsupported and never as verified. Verifiers keep accepting every version they have ever accepted, so old receipts stay verifiable indefinitely.

10. Capture

A receipt can only be as complete as the path that produced it. Agents reach the system through the Model Context Protocol (MCP) [9], an open protocol by which AI tools connect to external tools. Tested clients include Claude Code; Claude Desktop, Cursor, Windsurf, VS Code, Codex CLI and Gemini CLI use the same standard configuration. There are three ways to capture (Figure 9).

In tool mode the agent calls cool_record_change after a meaningful action. In proxy mode, CooL sits between the agent and another MCP server and records every call that passes through, with its outcome, duration and digests of arguments and result. In hook mode, the editor reports file edits, and edits to prompt, agent, policy and model-configuration files become receipts. The components are arranged as in Figure 10. Everything except the reader runs on the producer's own infrastructure, and nothing is sent to any vendor.

Two engineering properties matter for evidence. First, the recorder never fails silently. A receipt that cannot be written is reported to the agent and the user, and the proxy appends the failure to the tool result rather than dropping it. Second, the log survives crashes. Receipts are appended with fsync under a cross-process lock. A line torn by a crash is set aside and reported, and numbering continues from the last complete receipt.

11. Authorization

Evidence of an action is most useful when it also shows the authority behind it. Five signed protocol objects provide this. Each has its own domain label and is verified against a purpose in the trust snapshot.

Principal. An identity issuer asserts that a principal exists (a human, workload or agent) and which keys it holds. Status changes are signed events, so a verifier can reconstruct the principal's state at the time of the action, not merely today. Delegation. A principal grants another a scope (actions, resource prefixes and an amount ceiling) for an audience and a validity window. Each link must be a subset of its parent, so privilege can never be amplified. Request. The agent states precisely what it wants to do, with a fresh nonce and an expiry. Its parameters are committed rather than disclosed. Decision. An evaluator applies a policy and signs permit, deny, indeterminate or error, bound to the request, the exact policy version and the facts used. Confirmation. The system that owns the action signs what it did.

A decision can be believed in two ways. A trusted decision asks the verifier to trust the evaluator's key. A reproducible decision asks for no trust at all. The policy language is deterministic and its evaluator public, so a verifier given the policy and the facts re-executes the evaluation and checks that it reaches the same result. When the policy changes later, the old decision still verifies against the version it actually used.

12. Enforcement

Evidence that an action was not permitted is of little comfort if it happened anyway. The decisive control is therefore placed in an enforcement gateway that sits with the tool, rather than in the agent. Before executing, the gateway checks:

  • that the action and resource match the tool, and the parameters open the request's commitment;
  • the decision, its signer and its binding to this request and policy;
  • the delegation chain;
  • that the workload now executing is the one that was authorised;
  • that the request's nonce is unused, consuming it atomically.

Any failure, and any check whose answer is unknown, produces a refusal. Whatever the outcome, the gateway signs a confirmation of what happened. The MCP integration of Section 10 records but does not block; deployments that need blocking route the consequential tools through the gateway.

13. Hardware Attestation

Where evidence is produced inside a trusted execution environment, the sealing key can be bound to the hardware's statement about what code is running (Figure 12). The sealer generates a fresh key inside the enclave and asks the platform to attest to it. On AWS Nitro Enclaves the attestation's user_data carries

u = SHA-256("cool.nitro.key-binding.v1\0" ‖ CBOR(id, pkML-DSA, pkEd25519))

On Intel TDX and AMD SEV-SNP the equivalent field is report_data. The verifier checks three things:

  • the attestation chains to the vendor root, which is pinned in code;
  • the measurements match a pinned policy;
  • the bound key is exactly the key that signed the evidence.

A valid result supports one claim: code with these measurements held this key. It does not show that the code is free of defects.

14. Verification and Honest Outcomes

Verification recomputes everything from the bytes, offline. A sealed bundle passes through the checks in Figure 13, and each check reports pass, fail or unknown, with a reason.

Tampering is caught where the arithmetic fails. If one byte of a receipt is changed, its recomputed digest d′ ≠ d, so the signature over d no longer verifies. This holds even when the attacker also updates the file's checksum in the manifest. If a receipt is removed, the signed manifest no longer matches. If proofs are swapped to reorder history, the inclusion proofs fail. If a receipt is duplicated, the duplicate check fails. Every result also carries, in words, what the evidence covers and what it cannot show (Section 16), so the limits travel with the verdict.

Collapsing all of this into one bit would destroy information. A missing witness is not a forgery, and an unknown platform is not a valid one. The core library therefore summarises each report with one of six outcomes, chosen by a fixed precedence:

invalid  >  unsupported  >  stale  >  incomplete  >  indeterminate  >  verified

OutcomeMeaningTypical cause
invalida check failedaltered bytes, untrusted signer, duplicate, reorder

staletrue once, not shown true nowexpired attestation, old trust snapshot

The command-line verifier for agent receipts reports these as three verdicts with exit codes: VALID (0) for verified, INVALID (1) for invalid or incomplete, and INDETERMINATE (2) otherwise.

15. Privacy

Evidence must be shareable without disclosing the data it describes. Agent inputs and outputs are reduced to keyed digests under a random key that never leaves the producer:

input_digest = HMAC-SHA-256k("cool.mcp.input.v1\0" ‖ canonical_json(input))

Because the hash is keyed, a holder of the bundle cannot test a guess ("was the input X?") by hashing it. The producer can later prove a match by disclosing the input and the key to the person who needs to see them. Authorization parameters use salted or keyed commitments over normalised values, which can be opened selectively in the same way.

The log never needs the content; it stores digests. Envelope bodies can therefore be deleted under a retention policy while their leaves stay in the tree, and the history remains provable (Figure 14). Each deletion is itself a signed envelope, and a later export reports the deleted body as missing, so a verifier sees incomplete rather than a clean history.

16. Threat Model and the Capture Boundary

Against a sealed bundle, the following are detected. Each case is an automated test that runs on every change: a changed receipt (even with its file checksum updated), a receipt removed from the middle, reordered proofs, a duplicated or replayed receipt, a corrupted signature, a removed inclusion proof, a missing or corrupt file, and a bundle signed by a different producer. A bundle presented without an independently obtained key is indeterminate.

What a recorder that runs beside a system cannot see is equally important. We call this the capture boundary (Figure 15).

Inside the boundary, three limits remain, and each has a mitigation:

  • Removed final receipts. Removing the final receipts before sealing leaves no gap. A witnessed or anchored tree size closes this.
  • A rewritten history. A producer with full control of its machine can construct a new, internally consistent history. Witnesses that hold earlier tree heads detect this, as do recipients who keep earlier bundles.
  • The recorder's clock. Timestamps come from the recorder unless externally anchored.

Assurance rises in steps:

  1. Route consequential tools through the proxy.
  2. Publish fingerprints and tree sizes outside the organisation.
  3. Add trusted timestamps.
  4. Hold keys in a KMS or HSM.
  5. Run the recorder in an attested enclave.
  6. Place the gateway in front of the tool, so that what bypasses approval cannot execute.

CooL is tamper-evident, not tamper-proof. It covers actions recorded through it, not every action. It is evidence for controls, not a compliance certificate.

17. Calculations

Forgery.

To forge a receipt, an adversary must produce a valid ML-DSA-65 signature and a valid Ed25519 signature under keys the verifier trusts. Against a classical adversary the work is at least that of breaking Ed25519, about 2128 operations. Against an adversary with a large quantum computer, Ed25519 falls but ML-DSA-65 (NIST security category 3) still stands. The hybrid is never weaker than its stronger component. Substituting keys does not help either. Matching a published 256-bit fingerprint with different keys is a second-preimage attack on SHA-256, with work about 2256.

Equivocation.

Suppose a log shows a victim a forked history. The fork is discovered if any party compares the victim's tree head with the head the rest of the world sees. If each of z independent relying parties makes that comparison against an honest witness with probability q, the fork survives all of them with probability

P = (1 − q)z

double ForkSurvives(double q, int z)
{
    double p = 1.0;
    for (int i = 0; i < z; i++)
        p *= (1.0 - q);
    return p;
}

A verifier that demands co-signatures from t witnesses forces q = 1 for itself, unless the adversary also controls those t witnesses.

Guessing payloads.

A keyed digest cannot be tested without the key. With the key withheld, no guess can be checked at all, whatever the size of the space. With an unkeyed hash, a refund amount of up to 106 cents would fall to a million guesses; this is why the keyed form is the default.

Undetected omission.

Dropping a numbered receipt that is followed by another is detected with certainty by any verifier that sees the later one. Only receipts after the last one observed by an outside party can vanish unnoticed. If a producer seals and anchors every h seconds, an omission can stay hidden for at most h seconds plus the delivery delay.

Proof and bundle size.

An inclusion proof for n receipts has ⌈log2 n⌉ hashes of 32 bytes: 320 bytes for a thousand receipts. A sealed receipt carries its own ML-DSA-65 signature (3,309 bytes) and the tree head's (another 3,309 bytes) as base64 text. Measured bundles are about 20 KB per receipt (Section 18), which a verifier can check in milliseconds per receipt.

Replay.

Each authorization request carries a fresh nonce and an expiry, and the gateway consumes the nonce atomically before executing. Of any number of concurrent submissions of one authorization, at most one executes. This is tested with 25 parallel submissions on one host, and with competing clients against a shared Redis store. Within an agent receipt log, numbers are assigned under a cross-process lock. Three concurrent recorders writing 75 receipts produce no repeated or skipped number.

20. Status and Limitations

A system that asks to be trusted for honesty about evidence must be honest about itself. Every capability carries exactly one label, defined in code and enforced by the test suite. Implemented means validated against genuine evidence or a real service. Experimental means built and tested, but not yet validated against the real thing.

Some limits are inherent to the approach and will not change:

  • CooL proves that a record is authentic, consistent and complete within its sequence. It does not prove that the recorded statement is true, or that a decision was wise.
  • Actions outside the capture boundary, or that bypass the gateway, are not covered.
  • An issuer or evaluator that the verifier trusts can lie within its authority.
  • Attestation shows a measured launch state, not correct behaviour.
  • Witness independence is operational.

Each of these limits is reported explicitly in the verification output.

21. Conclusion

We have proposed an evidence system for consequential actions that does not rely on trust in the operator. We started from receipts: canonical records committed by domain-separated hashes and hybrid post-quantum signatures, numbered without gaps. Signatures and numbers alone cannot reveal equivocation, so every receipt becomes a leaf of a Merkle log whose tree heads can be witnessed and anchored in external time. A bundle cannot vouch for its own keys, so the key fingerprint travels by a separate channel. To connect evidence to authority, we added a protocol in which identity, delegation, request and decision are each signed by the party responsible, and enforcement sits with the tool. To make this available where software now acts most, AI agents produce receipts through the Model Context Protocol, by explicit call, through a recording proxy, or through editor hooks. Verification runs offline, gives a reason for every check and never reports a gap as success. The result does not make software trustworthy. It makes its record checkable, by anyone, without asking permission.
